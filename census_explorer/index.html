How to read it

  • Metro areas are the Census Bureau's metropolitan statistical areas as defined in 2023: a core city of at least 50,000 people plus the surrounding counties tied to it by commuting. Puerto Rico's metros are not included. Some regions are split into several metros, so Ogden and Provo are separate from Salt Lake City and Boulder from Denver.
  • Growth, migration, children and age come from the Census Bureau's yearly estimates (July 1, 2010 to 2025), added up from each metro's counties. Net moves count people arriving from other U.S. counties minus people leaving, per 1,000 residents per year, averaged over the years ending July 2021 to 2025. Moves between counties of the same metro cancel out.
  • Turnover, households and housing come from the American Community Survey, which pools five years of responses: 2020–24 and 2009–13. "Moved in recently" means since 2020 in the 2020–24 survey and since 2010 in the 2009–13 survey, which cover different spans of time, so there is no change view for it. "20+ years" means since before 2000 (or before 1990). "Typical years in the home" is the middle of the survey period minus the median year households moved in.
  • Bachelor’s or higher is the share of adults 25 and older whose highest schooling is a bachelor’s, master’s, professional or doctoral degree (survey table B15003). It counts people, not households. The change view compares the 2009–13 and 2020–24 surveys in percentage points.
  • Neighborhoods are 2020 census tracts, about 4,000 people each. The 2009–13 survey used 2010 tracts; its figures are moved onto 2020 tracts by shared land area, and medians (age, home value, income) are averaged, so treat the then-and-now comparison for a single neighborhood as approximate. Survey figures for one neighborhood carry wide margins of error; patterns across several neighborhoods are more reliable than any one.
  • Homelessness comes from HUD’s Point-in-Time count: one night each January, run by local Continuums of Care (CoCs), counting people in shelters and transitional housing and people sleeping outside. Counts are assigned to each metro’s counties by population. Many CoCs cover more than one metro (especially statewide “balance of state” CoCs), so for metros marked * the figure is the wider area’s rate rather than a count of that metro alone. The count misses people doubled up with friends or family, and unsheltered counts depend on volunteer effort, so treat small differences loosely. Shelter counts in New York, Denver, Chicago and a few other cities rose in 2023–25 partly because of newly arrived migrant families.
  • Neighborhood safety (Seattle and Denver only, inside city limits) uses each city’s police offense records from January 2023 through September 2026, averaged per year. Violent crime here means homicide, robbery and aggravated assault; sexual assaults are left out because Denver does not publish them. Property crime means burglary, theft (including from cars), car theft and arson. Rates per 1,000 residents overstate danger in places with many visitors and few residents (downtowns, shopping areas, stations), so each measure is also shown per square mile; neighborhoods with fewer than 500 residents get no per-resident rate. Encampment reports are Seattle Find It, Fix It requests: they count reports, not encampments, and some neighborhoods report much more than others. Pedestrian and cyclist injury crashes are Seattle police collision reports in which a walker or rider was involved and someone was hurt or killed; Denver’s crash file was not available.
  • Colors. List cells are shaded by where the metro falls among the metros shown, darker for higher values in both light and dark themes; this marks size, not good or bad. On the map, levels are shaded the same way, darker for higher; by default both maps share one color scale, set from the 2nd to the 98th percentile of the two metros' neighborhoods together (5th to 95th for changes), so the same color means the same value on both sides; switch Colors compare to Each metro to scale each map on its own; changes use red for decreases and blue for increases, and the most extreme neighborhoods take the end color. Home values and incomes are in dollars of each period, not adjusted for inflation.
